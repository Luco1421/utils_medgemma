\gls{MedVQA} has emerged as a promising research area at the intersection of computer vision, natural language processing, and healthcare. The objective of \gls{MedVQA} is to automatically answer clinically relevant questions based on medical images, enabling more effective access to visual information and supporting healthcare professionals in diagnostic and decision-making processes. Recent advances in multimodal foundation models have significantly improved the capability of systems to jointly reason over visual and textual information, leading to notable progress in medical image understanding and question answering tasks.

Among these models, MedGemma has recently demonstrated strong performance across a variety of medical multimodal benchmarks by leveraging large-scale pretraining and instruction tuning. However, despite these advances, medical visual question answering remains challenging due to the complexity of medical imagery, the presence of subtle pathological findings, and the need for precise localization of clinically relevant regions. Conventional multimodal models typically process entire images, which may introduce irrelevant visual information and reduce the model's ability to focus on diagnostically important structures.

Segmentation techniques offer a potential solution by explicitly highlighting regions of interest within medical images. The \gls{SAM} and its medical adaptations have shown remarkable capabilities in generating accurate segmentation masks with limited supervision. By isolating relevant anatomical structures or pathological regions, segmentation can guide multimodal models toward more focused visual reasoning. Furthermore, textual descriptions conditioned on segmented regions can provide complementary semantic information, enriching the visual representation with clinically meaningful context.

Motivated by these observations, this work proposes Multimodal MedGemma, a framework that integrates \gls{SAM}-based segmented medical images and segmentation-conditioned textual descriptions as multimodal inputs for \gls{MedVQA}. The proposed approach aims to enhance the visual grounding capabilities of MedGemma by providing both localized visual evidence and structured semantic context. In particular, we investigate the effectiveness of combining segmented visual regions with automatically generated descriptions that are conditioned on the identified anatomical or pathological areas.

Additionally, the study explores data-efficient adaptation strategies for MedGemma under limited labeled data scenarios, which are common in medical domains due to annotation costs and privacy constraints. We evaluate the impact of fine-tuning MedGemma compared with using the pretrained model directly, as well as the influence of different segmentation pipelines based on \gls{SAM} with \gls{LoRA}. Specifically, we compare segmentation obtained from the highest-confidence mask selection strategy against an Automatic Mask Generator combined with a mask selection mechanism.

The main objective of this research is to determine whether segmentation-guided multimodal inputs can improve the reasoning capabilities of MedGemma for \gls{MedVQA} tasks while maintaining strong performance in low-resource settings. To this end, the study addresses the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}

  \item How does MedGemma perform when provided with multimodal inputs consisting of \gls{SAM}-segmented medical images and segmentation-conditioned textual descriptions for Medical Visual Question Answering?

  \item What is the impact of fine-tuning MedGemma with limited labeled data compared to using the pretrained model without additional training?
\end{enumerate}

By answering these questions, this work contributes to the growing body of research on multimodal medical foundation models and provides insights into the integration of segmentation-guided visual representations and contextual descriptions for more accurate and interpretable medical question answering systems.

\subsection{Ophthalmic Image Segmentation}

Ophthalmic image segmentation remains constrained by the high cost of acquiring pixel-wise annotations in clinical settings, a challenge extensively documented by \hl{Wang et al.} \cite{wang2021annotation_efficient}. \hl{The Retinal Fundus Glaucoma Challenge (REFUGE)} benchmark defined an influential evaluation protocol for optic disc and cup segmentation under realistic class imbalance, establishing the dataset used in this work \cite{orlando2020refuge}. \hl{Foundation models, particularly the Segment Anything Model and its derivatives, have subsequently attracted considerable interest for their prompt-based, zero-shot generalization.} A systematic study by \hl{Huang et al} confirmed that SAM achieves satisfactory outcomes on targets with clear boundaries but degrades significantly on amorphous or low-contrast lesions, especially in the absence of manual prompts \cite{huang2024sam_medical}. \hl{Zhang et al.} extended this picture with a comprehensive survey of \hl{SAM's} current applications and future directions in medical image segmentation, highlighting the persistent domain gap between natural-image pretraining and clinical photographic data \cite{zhang2024sam_survey}. \hl{Ma et al.}\ addressed this gap by releasing \hl{MedSAM,} fine-tuned on over 1.5 million medical image-mask pairs, demonstrating improved robustness across \hl{CT, MRI,} and histopathology \cite{ma2024medsam}. In the ophthalmic domain specifically, \hl{Li et al.}\ adapted SAM for multi-modal retinal anomaly detection via a modality-shared decoder with task-specific tokens, illustrating that task-oriented fine-tuning is necessary to handle the unique photometric characteristics of fundus and OCT imaging \cite{li2025adapting}. Ali et al.\ provided a comprehensive review of SAM adaptations in medical imaging more broadly, consolidating findings from the past two years and outlining remaining challenges \cite{ali2025sam_review}. Building directly on these foundations, Ibba et al.\ proposed a weakly supervised pipeline leveraging Grad-CAM and SAM-based mask selection for anterior lens cataract segmentation \cite{ibba_weakly_2025}, Abreu-Cárdenas et al.\ benchmarked weak supervision, few-shot learning and LoRA-adapted SAM under limited annotations \cite{abreu-cardenas_benchmark_2025}, and Cárdenas et al.\ demonstrated few-shot cataract detection via kernel density estimation on SAM embeddings \cite{cardenas_few-shot_2025}.

\subsection{Medical Large Language Models}
The adaptation of large language models to clinical tasks has advanced rapidly, progressing from domain-specific pretraining to multimodal reasoning over combined textual and visual inputs. A foundational milestone was established by Singhal et al., who introduced Med-PaLM and demonstrated that instruction-tuned LLMs can encode clinical knowledge at levels approaching expert physicians, setting a benchmark for medical language understanding \cite{singhal2023medpalm}. The theoretical basis for resource-efficient adaptation was subsequently formalized by Ding et al., who showed that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) preserves general model capabilities while enabling task-specific specialization with orders-of-magnitude fewer trainable parameters \cite{ding2023peft}. A comprehensive empirical audit by Dutt et al.\ evaluated 17 PEFT algorithms across six medical imaging datasets and over 700 controlled experiments, establishing PEFT as particularly effective in the low-data regimes characteristic of clinical practice, with performance gains of up to 22\% \cite{dutt2024peft_midl}. In the ophthalmic domain, a systematic review by Thirunavukarasu et al.\ surveyed the growing literature on LLM evaluations in ophthalmology and found that while LLMs are approaching expert-level performance on text-based clinical questions, multimodal tasks and non-English contexts remain substantially underexplored \cite{thirunavukarasu2025llm_ophthalmology}. More recently, Google Research released MedGemma, a family of multimodal foundation models built on the Gemma 3 architecture, reporting improvements of 2.6--10\% on multimodal medical question answering and 15.5--18.1\% on chest X-ray classification compared to the base model, making it the strongest openly accessible baseline for medical multimodal reasoning at the time of this work \cite{sellergren2025medgemma}.

\subsection{Medical Visual Question Answering (MedVQA)}
\hl{Medical Visual Question Answering (MedVQA)} has emerged as a paradigmatic multimodal task that jointly demands image understanding and clinical reasoning.\hl{ Lin et al.\ }provided the first comprehensive survey of the field, categorizing datasets, architectures, and evaluation metrics, and identifying visual grounding and interpretability as the two most persistent open challenges \cite{lin2023medvqa_survey}. \hl{The dominant limitation across architectures is that models process entire images, without attending explicitly to diagnostically relevant regions, which degrades answer accuracy in the presence of localized pathologies.} A large-scale dataset effort by \hl{Li et al.} \hl{(PMC-VQA)} demonstrated that scale and instruction tuning are critical drivers of generalization, achieving state-of-the-art performance across diverse medical imaging modalities \cite{li2024pmcvqa}. A recent review by \hl{Pellegrini et al.} systematically documented the transition from discriminative, closed-vocabulary models toward generative vision-language architectures in medical report generation and \hl{VQA}, highlighting how cross-modal alignment strategies increasingly approximate clinical narrative quality \cite{pellegrini2024vlm_review}. \hl{Thirunavukarasu et al.} further showed, through head-to-head comparisons in ophthalmology, that state-of-the-art \hl{LLMs} are approaching expert-level performance on text-based clinical reasoning while remaining below standard on image-dependent questions \cite{thirunavukarasu2024llm_ophthalm}. These observations collectively motivate the segmentation-guided approach proposed in this work: by providing a model with localized visual regions derived from \hl{SAM} rather than entire images, the visual grounding bottleneck is directly addressed at the input level.

\subsection{Foundation Segmentation Models in Healthcare}
\hl{Foundation segmentation models have fundamentally altered the paradigm in medical image analysis by enabling prompt-driven, zero-shot segmentation without task-specific training}. \hl{Huang et al.} conducted a rigorous multi-modality evaluation of SAM on 17 medical imaging datasets, confirming that while the model performs well on targets with distinct boundaries, performance degrades substantially on irregular lesions and in the absence of explicit user prompts \cite{huang2024sam_medical}. \hl{Zhang et al.} systematically surveyed current applications of SAM in medical image segmentation, covering empirical benchmarking, methodological adaptations, and open research directions, providing the most comprehensive state-of-the-art picture at the time of this work \cite{zhang2024sam_survey}.\hl{ Ma et al.} introduced MedSAM, demonstrating that fine-tuning \hl{SAM} on a curated corpus of over 1.5 million image-mask pairs yields markedly improved robustness across \hl{CT, MRI}, and histopathology, while retaining zero-shot generalization to novel anatomies \cite{ma2024medsam}. \hl{Ali et al.} extended this analysis with a systematic review of SAM adaptations developed over 2023--2024, assessing multimodal fusion frameworks, volumetric extensions, and domain adaptation strategies, and identifying parameter-efficient fine-tuning as the dominant adaptation paradigm \cite{ali2025sam_review}. In the weakly supervised direction most relevant to this work, \hl{Wang et al.} proposed WeakMedSAM, which combines sub-class exploration with prompt affinity mining to refine class activation maps, enabling weakly supervised segmentation without pixel-level annotations across brain tumor and cardiac \hl{MRI} datasets \cite{wang2025weakmedsam}. Collectively, these works establish the boundary of current capability and motivate the integration of \hl{SAM} with MedGemma's visual reasoning in this work.

\subsection{Image Captioning and Vision-to-Language Mapping}
\hl{Automated image captioning in the medical domain has progressed from encoder-decoder architectures producing fixed radiology summaries toward flexible vision-language models capable of generating open-ended, contextually rich clinical descriptions.} \hl{Dalla Serra et al.} demonstrated that combining an attention-based captioning model with GPT-3 and structured patient metadata substantially improves the semantic accuracy of generated radiology reports \cite{dalla_serra2023captioning}. A major advance in clinical captioning came with the work of \hl{Tanno et al.}, who built Flamingo-CXR, a large vision-language model for chest X-ray report generation, and evaluated it via expert radiologist panels; their results showed that AI-generated reports were rated as equivalent or preferable to clinician-written reports in 77.7\% of in/out patient cases, establishing a landmark for clinical deployment readiness \cite{tanno2025flamingo}. A comprehensive review by\hl{Pellegrini et al.} documented the broader landscape of vision-language models for medical report generation and VQA, noting that cross-modal alignment remains the primary bottleneck for report quality \cite{pellegrini2024vlm_review}.\hl{Conditioning report generation on segmented regions of interest has been identified as a promising mechanism to improve visual grounding: rather than describing entire images, models that attend to localized anatomical or pathological structures produce more specific and clinically relevant narratives.} \hl{Selvaraju et al.} formalized \hl{gradient-weighted class activation maps (Grad-CAM)} \hl{as an explainability tool that can serve precisely this localization function, providing the prior that SAM then refines into segmentation masks in the pipeline proposed in this work }\cite{selvaraju2020gradcam}. The integration of such segmentation-conditioned descriptions as multimodal input to a downstream MedVQA model constitutes the core architectural contribution of the current paper.

The MM-ODIR-129 dataset comprises color fundus photographs exclusively sourced from the ODIR-5K public repository\cite{odir2019}, a widely used benchmark for retinal image analysis. The subset was curated to include a balanced representation of healthy and glaucomatous eyes.

The cohort encompasses \textbf{129 fundus images} that, based on the ODIR-5K file naming convention (\texttt{\{identifier\}\_\{left$|$right\}.jpg}), correspond to \textbf{76 unique identifiers} shared between left and right eyes. Notably, 12 identifiers present a mixed-diagnosis profile, with one eye classified as normal and the contralateral eye as glaucomatous, enabling within-subject comparison of pathological versus healthy structural changes. The ODIR-5K naming convention suggests that each numeric identifier corresponds to a unique patient; therefore, the dataset is described as \textbf{129 images from 76 patients}. A defining feature of the dataset is its heterogeneous spatial resolution, a direct consequence of the un-preprocessed image structure of the root ODIR-5K dataset. We identified 15 distinct resolution configurations across the 129 images. This resolution variability is preserved intentionally in the dataset to maintain pixel-level fidelity and to enable geometric cup-to-disc ratio (CDR) computation at the native scale of each acquisition. No global resizing or standardization was applied to the original captures prior to annotation.

All images were reviewed and labeled by five board-certified ophthalmologists, each of them present as authors of the present paper. The labeling process was conducted through the \textbf{OphthalmoCapture-V1} web-based annotation platform, a custom tool designed for structured ophthalmic data collection. The platform enforces a multi-step annotation pipeline: (i) binary disease classification (Normal vs.~Pathological), (ii) condition-specific structured scoring (seven glaucoma signs), and (iii) free-text clinical transcription. The annotation sessions were tracked via unique session hashes and timestamped to ensure auditability.

Pixel-level segmentation of the optic disc and optic cup was performed independently using \textbf{ImageJ} (National Institutes of Health, USA), following a manual delineation protocol performed by the same ophthalmologists. These tracings were then exported as binary masks and stored as NumPy arrays for downstream computational use. Normal images were not segmented, as the primary objective of the masks is to quantify structural deformation in pathological cases.

Each sign is encoded on an ordinal Likert scale reflecting increasing severity or presence, grounded in standard glaucoma diagnostic criteria. The seven fields collectively define a \textbf{structural sign vector} that characterizes the visible glaucomatous damage profile of each eye.

\paragraph{Segmentation Masks: }

Pixel-level segmentation masks for the optic disc and optic cup are provided for all 69 glaucomatous images, the 60 normal images do not include masks. The masks were generated through expert manual tracing in \textbf{ImageJ}. These tracings were exported as binary NumPy arrays (\texttt{uint8}, values $\{0, 1\}$) and stored alongside PNG visualizations for qualitative inspection. Both cup and disc masks preserve the native resolution of the original fundus capture, enabling precise area-based CDR computation at the original scale without interpolation artifacts.

$\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{Grad-CAM}}$ is the intersection-over-union between the
thresholded Grad-CAM saliency map and the ground-truth optic disc mask, quantifying
whether the model attends to the clinically relevant region; and
$\mathrm{VRAM}_{\text{norm}}$ is the peak inference GPU memory, min--max normalized
across the compared backbones. All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU (32\,GB). \\

% Párrafo 3: describir brevemente Meta-Learning. (dar contexto sobre por qué Meta Learning se consideró como configuración a comparar)
Meta-learning trains the backbone episodically---each episode builds class prototypes from a support set and classifies a query set against them~\cite{snell2017prototypical}---so the embedding space is optimized for the few-shot decision itself. We included it because a frozen ImageNet backbone yields generic embeddings that are not specialized to fundus images or to the
prototype rule, whereas meta-learning adapts the features to make prototypes more separable from very few labels, making it a natural counterpart to the frozen configuration.

% Párrafo 4: describir la dinámica del experimento: explicar detalladamente las configuraciones comparadas (las seeds utilizadas y por qué, los N utilizados para calcular los prototipos y por qué) y terminar con una tabla que muestre los resultados del experimento. (descripción detallada del experimento)
\paragraph{Configurations and protocol:}
Each of the three backbones is evaluated under two configurations---a \emph{frozen}
feature extractor with Euclidean prototypes, and a \emph{meta}-trained variant---for
a total of six configurations that are ranked by the score of Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}. For a given seed, backbone, configuration, and support size $N$, a class-balanced
support set of $N$ glaucoma and $N$ normal images is sampled from the REFUGE
training split. 

In the \emph{meta} configuration the backbone is first episodically
meta-trained on the training split: each of $100$ episodes draws a $2$-way task with
$N$ support and $10$ query images per class, forms class prototypes from the
support, and minimizes the cross-entropy of the nearest-prototype classification of
the query with respect to the backbone (Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, with data
augmentation). The \emph{frozen} configuration skips this step and uses the
ImageNet-pretrained backbone as is. In both cases the two class prototypes are then
computed as the mean support embedding per class, and the \emph{entire} validation
split is classified by nearest prototype under Euclidean distance, yielding the
macro-averaged F1. Grad-CAM localization (IoU against the optic disc mask,
thresholded at the $95$th percentile) and peak inference memory are measured on the
resulting model.

Because the target scenario is few-shot---only a handful of labeled fundus images
per class---we probe small, class-balanced support sizes $N\in\{1,\dots,5\}$ and
report, per configuration, the macro-F1 \emph{averaged over the five sizes}
($\overline{\mathrm{F1}}$); this rewards backbones that are robust across the
low-data regime rather than ones that peak at a single $N$. Since the support set is
drawn at random, each configuration is repeated over five fixed seeds
($\{42,123,456,789,1024\}$) and all metrics are averaged over them. The seed also
fixes \emph{which} images are sampled, and within a seed every backbone and
configuration receives the \emph{same} support, turning the comparison into a paired
one in which differences are attributable to the model rather than to a fortunate
draw; fixing the seeds additionally makes the experiment reproducible.
